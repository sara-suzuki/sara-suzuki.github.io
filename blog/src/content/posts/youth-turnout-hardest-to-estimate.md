Voter turnout, and by extension turnout of key groups in our electorate like youth, seems like a number we should be able to know precisely.

Yet when [CIRCLE released](https://circle.tufts.edu/latest-research/2026-youth-voter-turnout-second-highest-last-30-years) its week-of estimate that 27% of young people ages 18–29 voted in the 2026 midterms, we reported it with a margin of error of plus or minus two points, and we expect to update it in the Spring with a new estimate based on voter file data.

The gap between how simple turnout seems and how hard it is to estimate is not only a technical problem, but an equity problem. The groups who face the most barriers to voting—young people, specific racial and ethnic groups (especially those who are a minority in their area), rural communities, and people who move often—are also the groups whose turnout is hardest to estimate.

That matters because practitioners need detailed numbers. Organizers deciding where to knock doors and funders deciding where to invest need turnout by age, race/ethnicity, gender, and place, often down to the county or precinct. Immediately after every election, practitioners (and journalists!) reach out asking for youth turnout by state or for specific groups of young people. As the next cycle ramps up, the requests become more granular: by county, by race and gender, or for 18- and 19-year-olds. Too often, our answer is some version of: the number doesn't exist, or any estimate we could produce carries too much uncertainty to share responsibly. These are people doing exactly the work our research is meant to support, so it never feels good to give that answer.

At CIRCLE, and across the civic engagement research field, we do our best with the data available when it comes to turnout statistics. In this post, I walk through where turnout numbers come from, where and why each source struggles, and how to read any single number.

## First, four numbers that get confused

Before getting to data sources, it helps to separate four numbers that often get conflated with each other:

- **Turnout** is the share of eligible people in a group who voted: youth turnout = youth votes ÷ eligible young people.
- **\[Group\] share** of the electorate is the share of all votes cast by that specific group: youth share = youth votes ÷ all votes.
- **Registration rate** is the share of eligible people who are registered: youth registration rate = registered young people ÷ eligible young people.
- **Yield** is the share of registered people who voted: youth votes ÷ registered young people.

Turnout is the product of the last two: turnout = registration rate × yield. That formula is useful for practitioners because it shows where the bottleneck is. A low registration rate with a high yield points to registration work. A high registration rate with a low yield points to mobilization.

Youth share does not predict youth turnout, and the two can even move in opposite directions. In 2026, for example, the youth share of voters was 11%, down from 12% in 2022, while CIRCLE's week-of turnout estimates for the two years were the same at 27%.

## The denominator: who counts as eligible?

The denominator seems like the easy part, and for the country as a whole, it mostly is. Researchers choose among four denominators: the voting-age population (everyone 18 and older), the citizen voting-age population (CVAP), the voting-eligible population (VEP), and registered voters. [Michael McDonald's VEP](https://election.lab.ufl.edu/voter-turnout/) narrows CVAP further by removing people disenfranchised by a felony conviction, but it isn't available by age. So for youth turnout estimates, most researchers use CVAP for 18–29-year-olds from the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS).

Using the ACS as a source for the denominator means [choosing between 1-year and 5-year estimates](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2019/acs/ACS_rural_handbook_2019_ch02.pdf), and each comes with real tradeoffs.

1-year estimates draw on 12 months of data, so they are the most current and better capture populations that change quickly, which matters for an age group that turns over every year. But they are published only for places with at least 65,000 people, and their smaller samples carry larger margins of error. Most counties, including nearly all rural counties, fall below the 65,000 threshold and have only 5-year estimates.

5-year estimates pool 60 months of data, cover every geography, and have smaller margins of error. The tradeoff is that there is a considerable time lag. For example, at the time of the 2026 midterms, the most recent 5-year estimates covered 2020–2024 and had been released only in late January 2026.

Across both types of data, denominators that are more granular carry more error. For example, the count of Black youth citizens for a specific state often carries considerable uncertainty. Many of the denominators we want aren't in standard tables at all, so researchers must build their own estimates using the ACS Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS).

## The numerator, part 1: vote counts × youth share

In the days after an election, CIRCLE estimates youth turnout by multiplying the number of ballots counted by the share of voters who were ages 18–29, according to election surveys, and dividing by the number of young citizens. Vote tallies are incomplete at that point, and in some states they stay incomplete for weeks. That creates a real tension between waiting for more data and reporting in a timely manner so that turnout data can be part of the election narrative.

The youth share comes from election surveys, and since 2018 there have been only two major sources. From 2018 through 2024, they were the Edison Research exit poll and AP VoteCast. In 2026, the sources changed, but there are still only two: [The Voter Poll by SSRS](https://ssrs.com/ssrs-solutions/voter-poll/) and NORC's [Civic Health and Elections Project](https://www.norc.org/research/library/norc-launches-civic-health-and-elections-project.html).

Small differences in the youth share matter a lot. If one survey puts the youth share at 11% and youth turnout works out to 27%, a true share of 12% would mean turnout of about 30%, and a true share of 13% would mean about 32%. In other words, a one- or two-point difference in the youth share moves the turnout estimate by roughly 2.5 to 5 points. Because this method depends so heavily on the youth share, it matters that the two sources don't always agree.

The problem grows for subgroups. Each survey may include only a few hundred young voters in a given state, so estimates for youth within a state, let alone for specific groups of young people, carry very large margins of error. That is why election-week youth turnout estimates for individual states don't exist.

## The numerator, part 2: asking people whether they voted

The Census Bureau's Current Population Survey (CPS) Voting and Registration Supplement is the only large federal survey of voting, and its consistency over decades makes it the best source for long-run trends. But it relies on people self-reporting whether they voted.

People overreport voting, partly because of social desirability bias, and this bias is not evenly spread among the population. For example, one study by [Ansolabehere and Hersh](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mps023) found that individuals who are "well-educated, high-income, partisan, politically active, \[and\] church-attending" were more likely to report voting when they had not. Additionally, one household member often answers for others, so a parent may be reporting whether their child voted.

The Census Bureau also counts people who don't answer the voting question as nonvoters. Researchers [recommend](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nft042) reweighting the data to address the nonresponse problem.

Regardless, the CPS is designed for national and state estimates. Young people of a particular race within a state quickly become small samples, and there are no county estimates at all.

## The numerator, part 3: voter files

Voter files record whether each registered person voted, which makes them the closest thing we have to a count. But a voter file is a living database, updated constantly as people register, move, and are removed from the rolls. This creates a "find the sweet spot" problem: pull the file too early, and it misses late registrants and vote history that states haven't added yet; pull it too late, and list maintenance has removed people who voted. You can see an illustration of this phenomenon in [Kim and Fraga's 2022 paper](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/api-gateway/apsa/assets/orp/resource/item/6321ef75faf4a4ba6f1213ef/original/when-do-voter-files-accurately-measure-turnout-how-transitory-voter-file-snapshots-impact-research-and-representation.pdf). Notably, they found these biases are largest for younger voters and voters of color.

Getting files straight from states is hard, too. [Prices can go up to $37,000 per state](https://ballotpedia.org/Availability_of_state_voter_files), and even if you were willing to pay, some states have restrictions on who can request them. For example, Illinois restricts requests to "registered political committees for bona fide political purposes."

Voter file vendors maintain and sell national voter files by collecting and standardizing voter files from all states. Researchers, including CIRCLE, rely on such databases, especially when reporting detailed turnout statistics such as for subgroups within youth.

However, the appended demographics are a real source of error and bias. When a state doesn't record someone's age, race, or gender, the voter file vendors fill in the blanks using proprietary models. They combine what's on the voter record, like a name and address, with commercially available data, such as consumer records and Census statistics about who lives in a neighborhood, to estimate the most likely answer. Even age is often modeled, because many states don't release full dates of birth in their voter files.

Race is modeled even more often, as only nine states ask registrants their race (several of them collect it to comply with the Voting Rights Act). Everywhere else, vendors model race largely from registrants' names and addresses. [Pew found](https://www.pewresearch.org/methods/2018/02/15/commercial-voter-files-and-the-study-of-u-s-politics/) these models classified race correctly for 93% of white panelists but only 72% of Hispanic panelists and 67% of Black panelists.

Gender is often modeled from first names, which forces everyone into a binary. Commercial files are also more likely to miss young, mobile, and Hispanic people entirely. In the Pew study, only 22% of panelists ages 18 to 29 could be found in all five files, compared with 59% of those 65 and older. Only 26% of Hispanic panelists were found in all five, compared with 47% of non-Hispanic white panelists. The pattern is consistent: the smaller and more marginalized the group, the less reliable the estimate.

This way of modeling race is a mismatch with how race is reported in the denominator. In the ACS, people report their own race and can choose more than one. In most voter files, race is a single assignment, often via a model. Dividing one by the other assumes they describe the same people in the same way.

## Doing our best with what we have

Turnout seems like it should be a simple count, but it requires weighing real tradeoffs. You have to combine sources, report margins of error, and update estimates when better data arrive.

Some best practices:

- Know which ratio you're looking at—what is your numerator and what is your denominator?
- Treat immediate estimates after an election as provisional, and wait for voter-file-based estimates, especially when looking at detailed turnout for specific groups and comparing groups.
- Look into how the demographics were created, and if using a voter file, know when the data was refreshed.
- Know the margin of error around your turnout number—error can live in both the numerator and denominator!
